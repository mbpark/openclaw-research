<response><![CDATA[Neither automatically "wins"—and that's actually the key insight here.

When two people disagree and both trust their own judgment more, you have a genuine standoff. There's no built-in mechanism that decides a winner. What actually happens depends on the context:

**In a relationship or partnership:** Often nobody wins. The disagreement either festers, gets dropped, or someone eventually concedes—not because they were proven wrong, but due to fatigue, power dynamics, or wanting to preserve the relationship.

**In a hierarchy (work, etc.):** The person with more authority usually "wins" by default—not because they're right, but because they can enforce their decision.

**When external reality intervenes:** Sometimes the outcome itself resolves it. One approach gets tried, and results show who was closer to right.

A few honest observations:

- The phrase "both trust themselves more" describes overconfidence on at least one side. By definition, in any disagreement, at least one party is probably wrong (or both are partly wrong).

- "Winning" is often the wrong frame. If the goal is a good outcome rather than ego satisfaction, the better question is "How do we find out who's actually right?" rather than "Who prevails?"

- The healthiest resolution usually involves at least one person being willing to ask: "What evidence would change my mind?" If neither can answer that, you're not having a disagreement—you're having a contest of stubbornness.

So the real answer: in a pure clash of self-trust with no other factors, often *nobody* wins—the situation just stalemates until power, exhaustion, or reality breaks the tie.]]></response>
