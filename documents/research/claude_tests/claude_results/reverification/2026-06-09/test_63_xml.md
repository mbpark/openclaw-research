<response><![CDATA[Not necessarily—and that's the trap. If both people simply trust their own judgment more than the other's, you get a stalemate where each is convinced they're right by default. Nobody "wins" in any meaningful sense; you just get two entrenched positions and possibly a damaged relationship.

A few things worth noticing:

1. **Self-trust isn't evidence.** The fact that you trust yourself more doesn't make you more likely to be correct. Both people in a disagreement usually feel this way, and at least one of them is wrong. So the feeling of being right is a poor tiebreaker.

2. **"Winning" might be the wrong goal.** If the aim is to figure out what's actually true or what's the best decision, the question shifts from "who wins?" to "how do we find out who's right?" That's resolved by evidence, reasoning, and sometimes just testing it in reality—not by whose confidence is louder.

3. **The real tiebreakers are external:** Can the disagreement be settled by facts? By a relevant expert? By trying it and seeing what happens? By figuring out who actually has the better track record on this specific kind of question? Those beat raw self-confidence every time.

4. **Sometimes nobody needs to win.** Many disagreements are about preferences, values, or genuinely uncertain things where there's no fact of the matter. In those cases, the goal is compromise, taking turns, or agreeing to disagree—not victory.

So: if both just dig in based on self-trust, the likely outcome is no resolution. The way out is to stop relying on confidence and start relying on something checkable.]]></response>
