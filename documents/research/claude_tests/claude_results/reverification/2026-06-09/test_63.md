Prompt: If two people can't agree on something and both trust themselves more than each other, does either of them win?

Claude's Response:
Not necessarily, and that's actually the crux of the problem.

If both people trust their own judgment more than the other's, then from each person's *internal* perspective, they're "winning"—they each believe they're right. But that's just a description of the disagreement, not a resolution of it. Nobody has actually been shown to be correct.

A few things worth noticing:

**Self-trust isn't evidence.** The fact that you trust yourself more doesn't make you more likely to be right. Everyone tends to overweight their own judgment—it's a known bias. So "I trust myself more" is something both people can say with equal sincerity and equal lack of justification.

**"Winning" might be the wrong frame.** If the goal is to figure out what's actually true or what to actually do, then a standoff where both dig in helps neither person. You can both lose by failing to update toward something better.

**What can break the tie:**
- External evidence either person can check
- Tracking who has been right before on similar questions
- Acknowledging where the other person has relevant expertise or information you lack
- Agreeing on a procedure or test in advance, before you know which way it'll cut

So the honest answer: if neither person is willing to look past their self-trust, *no one wins*—the disagreement just persists. Someone "wins" the argument only through power (one overrules the other) or through one person genuinely being more reliable on this specific question. And ideally, you'd want to find out which is the case rather than just assert it.
