Name: llm_deadlock
Prompt: If two people can't agree on something and both trust themselves more than each other, does either of them win?

Claude's Response:
Not necessarily—and that's actually the interesting part.

If both people trust themselves more than the other, you have a symmetric standoff. There's no built-in mechanism for either to "win," because the very thing that would resolve the disagreement (deferring to the more reliable judge) is exactly what each refuses to do. Trusting yourself more isn't evidence you're right; the other person has the identical feeling with equal conviction.

A few ways this actually plays out:

- **Neither wins** if winning means convincing the other. You just stay stuck. This is common and underrated—sometimes the honest outcome is unresolved disagreement.

- **One "wins" by power, not reason**—whoever has authority, controls the resource, or outlasts the other. But that's not the same as being correct.

- **Both lose** if the disagreement matters and the stalemate prevents action.

The escape hatch is usually to stop relying on self-trust as the tiebreaker and find something external: evidence you both accept, a test that could prove one of you wrong, a neutral third party, or a track record of who's been right about similar things before.

There's also a humbling asymmetry worth noticing: the *feeling* of being more trustworthy is something the wrong person experiences just as strongly as the right one. So "I trust myself more" can't be the final argument—it proves too much, since both can say it.

Is this abstract, or are you dealing with a specific disagreement? The right move depends a lot on whether it's a factual question, a values question, or a decision someone has to actually make.
